Why shipping companies in the UAE need ongoing legal support

The maritime law is recent. Federal Decree-Law No. 43 of 2023 Concerning the Maritime Law has applied since 29 March 2024 and repealed Federal Law No. 26 of 1981 (Article 368). It is the core of UAE shipping law, covering what other systems call admiralty and maritime law: registration, liens, arrest, crew, agents, charter parties, carriage, collisions, salvage, general average and marine insurance. Under Article 2 it applies without conflicting with the international agreements the State has ratified, so international maritime law still matters. Under Article 368, regulations made under the 1981 law continue, where consistent, until Executive Regulations are issued.

Time bars are short. Where the claim is denied and there is no lawful excuse, claims under a charter party are not heard after one year from its expiry (Article 136), and claims under a contract of carriage by sea are not heard after one year from delivery of the cargo or the date it should have been delivered (Article 187, which also sets a 90-day limit for recourse claims). How a period runs in a given case depends on the facts and needs checking with a lawyer.

Arrest moves fast. A ship may be arrested by order of the competent court for a maritime debt (Article 53). Article 59 gives the arresting party five working days to file its case on the validity of the arrest, failing which the arrest is void, and provides for a hearing within 15 days. The court work is outside the REGULEX subscription, but it helps owners and agents to know the steps in advance.

Licences sit at several levels. No ship agent may operate without the approval of the Ministry of Energy and Infrastructure and a licence from the competent authority, meaning the emirate's government or an entity affiliated to it (Articles 1 and 116); the same applies to cargo ("shipping") agents in UAE ports (Article 120). Registered ships need a navigation licence (Article 22). Foreign ships need Ministry approval to carry between UAE ports or to provide towing, supply and fuelling services in UAE waters (Article 15). In Dubai, the Dubai Maritime Authority regulates maritime activities, including in free zones, under Law No. 3 of 2023. In Abu Dhabi, Abu Dhabi Maritime registers maritime assets and licenses maritime activities. The conditions for each approval sit in implementing rules and need checking case by case.

Arbitration clauses age. Dubai Decree No. 34 of 2021 abolished the Emirates Maritime Arbitration Centre and transferred its rights and obligations to the Dubai International Arbitration Centre (DIAC). Contracts naming the old centre are worth a review.

The legal work that comes up every month

  • Charterparties. Time, voyage and bareboat charters. Article 131 of the Maritime Law requires a charter party to be in writing and lists what it must state, including the ship's IMO number, the freight and the loading and discharge terms.
  • Bills of lading and carriage terms. Standard terms, booking notes and letters of indemnity.
  • Agency agreements. Under Article 117 the ship agent is treated as the operator's representative in cases filed in the UAE by or against the operator, so indemnity and funding clauses matter.
  • Operations contracts. Ship management, crewing and seafarer employment contracts (Article 94), bunker supply, towage and repair.
  • Insurance wording. Review of hull, cargo and liability clauses, the work usually sent to marine lawyers or a marine insurance lawyer.
  • Port and free-zone paperwork. Terminal service agreements and leases.
  • Research memos. Whether a claim is a "maritime debt", which time bar applies, how a lien ranks.

Checklist before you sign a legal retainer

  • The legal retainer agreement lists the included work in writing.
  • It says plainly whether ship arrest and onshore court litigation are included or excluded.
  • It explains how urgent matters are handed over, since arrest deadlines run in days.
  • The fee is fixed, with VAT shown separately.
  • A lawyer reviews every document.
  • The provider's licence and licensing authority are stated.
  • Arbitration and DIFC or ADGM Court work has a separate quoting process.
  • The response time to a request is stated.
  • You can cancel on short notice.

FAQ

What is a retainer fee for a lawyer?

A recurring fee for agreed legal work over a period, instead of a bill per hour. Under the REGULEX subscription it is a fixed monthly fee from USD 990 plus 5% VAT.

What is maritime law in the UAE?

The main statute is Federal Decree-Law No. 43 of 2023, in force since 29 March 2024, with emirate and port rules around it. Searches for maritime admiralty law lead to the same statute.

Can a maritime lawyer on retainer arrest a ship?

Not under the REGULEX subscription. Arrest is ordered by the competent court (Articles 53 to 59 of the Maritime Law), and litigation before the UAE onshore courts is not included. The subscription covers contract review and a non-binding memo on the legal questions; the application itself needs court representation, which REGULEX does not provide. That is the service usually meant by searches for shipping lawyers in Dubai, a marine attorney, a shipping attorney or an admiralty law firm.

What happened to the Emirates Maritime Arbitration Centre?

Decree No. 34 of 2021 abolished it and transferred its assets, rights and obligations to DIAC. Arbitration agreements that named the centre before the decree remain valid, with DIAC in its place unless the parties agree otherwise (Article 6). DIAC publishes the DIAC Arbitration Rules 2022.

Who can register a ship under the UAE flag?

Article 13 of the Maritime Law requires, among other conditions, that the majority of the shares belong to UAE or GCC nationals, or to persons with a domicile, headquarters or ship management office in the UAE. It sets an age limit of 20 years, or 10 for passenger ships, and the Ministry of Energy and Infrastructure may refuse registration in the public interest. Registration is with the Ministry. Vessel registration is not a service REGULEX provides.
